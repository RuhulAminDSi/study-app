1de Broglie Waves (Matter Waves)

high-yield
Definition Louis de Broglie (1924, thesis; Nobel Prize 1937) turned Einstein's logic upside down: if light can behave like particles, then particles must behave like waves. Every moving particle of momentum p carries a wave of wavelength λ.
The one relation λ = h / p = h / mv = h / √(2mK)   (non-relativistic)
h = 6.626 × 10⁻³⁴ J·s (exact since the 2019 SI redefinition) · ħ = h/2π = 1.0546 × 10⁻³⁴ J·s
Situationλ
electron at 1 eV1.226 nm
electron through 100 V0.123 nm = 1.23 Å
electron through 10 kV0.123 pm
1 MeV proton or neutron28.6 fm
cricket ball, 0.1 kg at 10 m/s6.6 × 10⁻³⁴ m
Memory trick "Big thing → tiny wave." λ ∝ 1/p, so quantum behaviour shows up only for light or fast things.
Exam trap λ = h/p, not hp and not h/p². λ comes out in metres only if h is in J·s and p in kg·m/s. Above ~0.1c use p = γmv, not mv.
Worked examples ✍ (1) Electron at 2.0 × 10⁶ m/s: p = (9.11 × 10⁻³¹)(2.0 × 10⁶) = 1.82 × 10⁻²⁴ kg·m/s, so λ = 6.626 × 10⁻³⁴/1.82 × 10⁻²⁴ = 3.64 × 10⁻¹⁰ m = 0.364 nm.
(2) 1.0 MeV proton: p = √(2mE) = 2.32 × 10⁻²⁰ kg·m/s, so λ = 2.86 × 10⁻¹⁴ m = 28.6 fm — a nucleus-sized wavelength.
λC = h/mc: the λ of a particle at rest

Cross-reference: a photon also carries p = h/λ, but with zero rest mass so p = E/c. Davisson–Germer's confirmation (1927) and the Bohr condition 2πr = nλ sit on the Basic Physics de Broglie sheet.

2Phase Velocity & Group Velocity

MCQ favourite
Definition A free particle is not an endless wave — it is a wave packet: a superposition of many component waves of nearly equal k. Two speeds describe it, and only one of them is physical.
The two speeds

Phase velocity vp = ω/k = E/p — the speed of one single crest

Group velocity vg = dω/dk = dE/dp = v — the speed of the envelope, i.e. of the particle

Phase velocity vpGroup velocity vg
definitionω/k = E/pdω/dk = dE/dp
free massive particlec²/v (≥ c)= v (the particle)
light in vacuumcc
dispersive mediumω/ksignal & information speed
carriesno informationenergy and information
Derivation: vp·vg = c² ✍ Free particle: E² = p²c² + m²c⁴, so E = pc²/v. ① vp = E/p · ② vg = p²c²/E · ③ product = c². For light m = 0, E = pc, so both equal c.
Memory trick "One crest is a fiction, the packet is the truth." A lone crest carries no signal, so its speed is unmeasurable — and may exceed c.
Exam trap vp > c breaks nothing. vg is dE/dp, never dp/dm.
Worked example ✍ Electron at 0.8c: vg = 0.8c = 2.40 × 10⁸ m/s · vp = c²/(0.8c) = 1.25c = 3.75 × 10⁸ m/s · product 0.8 × 1.25 = 1, i.e. c² ✓
p = γmv = 3.64 × 10⁻²² kg·m/s → λ = 1.82 pm; using mv would have wrongly given 3.03 pm.

3The Wave Function Ψ & Born's Rule

MCQ + Written
Definition Ψ(x, y, z, t) is the complete mathematical description of a quantum state: it holds everything knowable about the particle. Ψ is complex-valued and is not itself measurable. Born's rule (1926) connects it to the laboratory: |Ψ|² is the probability density.
ObjectMeaning
Ψ(x, t)wave function — complex, unobservable
|Ψ|² = Ψ*Ψprobability density (per unit volume)
∫|Ψ|²dV= 1 (normalisation — exactly one particle)
|Ψ(x, t)|²dVprobability of finding it in the element dV
ψ, Eeigenfunction and eigenvalue of Ĥ
  • Single-valued, finite, continuous
  • Ψ and ∂Ψ/∂x continuous wherever V is finite
  • Ψ obeys superposition (it is linear)
  • Ψ ≡ 0 is not a state (cannot be normalised)
  • A measurement leaves Ψ in an eigenstate of that quantity
  • Two states add: Ψ = c₁Ψ₁ + c₂Ψ₂
Memory trick "Bar the star, then square": |Ψ|² = Ψ* × Ψ — multiply by the complex conjugate, never Ψ × Ψ.
Exam trap |Ψ|² is a density; a probability needs an integral. Ψ may be complex, so Ψ² can be complex and meaningless.
Worked example: normalisation ✍ ψ = A sin(πx/L) for 0 < x < L.
  1. 1 = ∫₀^L |A|²sin²(πx/L)dx = A²(L/2) ∫₀^L sin² = L/2
  2. A = √(2/L), so ψₙ = √(2/L) sin(nπx/L), n = 1, 2, 3 …
  3. Probability in the middle half (L/4 < x < 3L/4): P = (2/L)∫sin²dx = 1/2

PHY · Quantum MechanicsSchrödinger equation

4The Schrödinger Equation

written favourite
Definition The Schrödinger equation (1926; Nobel Prize 1933) is the fundamental law of quantum mechanics, playing the role Newton's second law plays classically. Given Ψ at one instant it fixes Ψ at every later instant. It does not say where the particle is — only how the state changes.
Both forms

Time-dependent (always valid):  iħ ∂Ψ/∂t = −(ħ²/2m)∇²Ψ + VΨ = ĤΨ

Time-independent (stationary states, V time-independent):  −(ħ²/2m)∇²ψ + Vψ = Eψ

In 1-D just replace ∇² by ∂²/∂x².

Classically we write……in quantum mechanics it becomes the operator…
position x · momentum px → x  ·  p → −iħ∇
kinetic energy p²/2m−(ħ²/2m)∇²
energy EE → iħ ∂/∂t
total energy HĤ = −(ħ²/2m)∇² + V (Hamiltonian operator)

Separation of variables

  1. Put Ψ(x, t) = ψ(x) T(t), substitute, divide through by ψT
  2. Left side depends on t, right side on position ⇒ both equal one constant, −E/ħ
  3. T(t) = e−iEt/ħ; since |T| = 1, |Ψ|² = |ψ|² is constant in time
  4. That is a stationary state; ψ is the eigenfunction, E the eigenvalue
Memory trick "Replace the word with a hat." Every quantity becomes an operator — p → −iħ∇, E → iħ ∂/∂t, H → Ĥ. Then the equation only says energy = Ĥ acting on the state.
Exam trap The time-independent form is invalid if V depends on time, and it uses lower-case ψ. A "stationary state" is stationary in probability only.
Worked example: free particle ✍ V = 0 gives −(ħ²/2m)d²ψ/dx² = Eψ. Try ψ = Aeikx, so d²ψ/dx² = −k²ψ:
−(ħ²/2m)(−k²)ψ = Eψ ⇒ E = ħ²k²/2m = h²/(2mλ²) = p²/2m — the Schrödinger equation hands back λ = h/p for a free particle.

PHY · Quantum MechanicsBox · oscillator

5Particle in a One-Dimensional Box

high-yield
The model V(x) = ∞ outside and V = 0 for 0 < x < L — infinitely hard walls. Because Ψ must stay finite and V = ∞ makes the particle impossible there, ψ(0) = ψ(L) = 0. Solving d²ψ/dx² = −(2mE/ħ²)ψ with those two boundary conditions forces the sine solution to fit a whole number of half-wavelengths.
QuantityResult
normalised eigenfunctionψn(x) = √(2/L) sin(nπx/L)
energy eigenvalueEn = n²π²ħ²/2mL² = n²h²/8mL²
allowed nn = 1, 2, 3 … (never 0); nodes = n − 1
levels in units of E₁1 : 4 : 9 : 16 …
momentum⟨p⟩ = 0, but ⟨p²⟩ = (nπħ/L)²
Memory trick A particle in a box is a string fixed at both ends. Only 3λ/2, 5λ/2, 7λ/2 … fit; λ, 2λ, 4λ never do. Hence n starts at 1 and E ∝ n².
Exam trap E₁ is not zero — it is the zero-point energy. And ⟨p⟩ = 0 does not mean p = 0; Δp is large (sheet 9).
Worked example: electron in a 0.1 nm box ✍ E₁ = h²/(8mL²) = (6.626 × 10⁻³⁴)²/(8 × 9.11 × 10⁻³¹ × 10⁻²⁰) = 6.02 × 10⁻¹⁸ J = 37.6 eV
E₂ = 150 eV · E₃ = 338 eV · E₄ = 602 eV. A 2 → 1 jump releases 3E₁ = 113 eV, so the emitted photon has λ = 1240/113 = 11.0 nm (far UV).
Halving L lifts every level fourfold

IT link: the barrier between two boxes is a quantum tunnel — the electron leaks through even when E < V. That is how NAND flash cells are programmed and erased, and how a scanning tunnelling microscope resolves single atoms (tunnelling current ∝ e−2κd, Binnig & Rohrer, Nobel 1986).

6The Quantum Harmonic Oscillator

MCQ + Written
Definition The 1-D bound system with a quadratic potential: V(x) = ½kx² = ½mω²x², where ω = √(k/m). It is the most exactly solvable system in quantum mechanics, and the model for molecular vibrations, lattice phonons and LC circuits.
Results

En = (n + ½)ħω = (n + ½)hf,   n = 0, 1, 2, 3 …

ψ0 = (mω/πħ)1/4e−mωx²/2ħ  ·  ⟨x⟩₀ = 0  ·  ⟨x²⟩₀ = ħ/(2mω)

Classical oscillatorQuantum oscillator
energiescontinuous, E = ½kA²discrete, (n + ½)ħω
lowest energymay be 0½ħω, never 0
spacingdepends on Aconstant, ħω
widthfixed amplitude AΔx₀ = √(ħ/2mω)
degeneracy (1-D)—none: every level is unique
Memory trick "The box counts from 1, the oscillator counts from 0." n = 0 would give the forbidden ψ ≡ 0 in the box; in the oscillator n = 0 is the real ground state with energy ½ħω.
Exam trap The oscillator is never dead still: ⟨p⟩ = 0 but ⟨p²⟩ ≠ 0. Its spacing does not grow with n, unlike the box.
Worked example ✍ A molecular vibration with ω = 5.0 × 10¹⁴ rad/s: ħω = (1.0546 × 10⁻³⁴)(5.0 × 10¹⁴) = 5.27 × 10⁻²⁰ J = 0.329 eV, so E₀ = 0.165 eV and E₁ − E₀ = 0.329 eV lands in the infrared. For an electron in the same field Δx₀ = √(ħ/2mω) = 0.34 nm — ten atomic diameters.

7The Hydrogen Atom & Bohr Quantisation

high-yield
Bohr's model (1913) Bohr kept Rutherford's nucleus but forced Planck's quantum on it. (1) The electron may move only in orbits whose angular momentum is a whole multiple of ħ — L = mev r = nħ. (2) In these stationary orbits it does not radiate; light appears only in jumps between orbits.

Memory trick "Two conditions, one spectrum." The orbit must hold a whole number of de Broglie wavelengths and have L = nħ; balancing the electrostatic pull against the centripetal need then forces rn = n²a₀.
Exam trap L = nħ is Bohr's, not Schrödinger's. Real QM gives L² = l(l+1)ħ² with l = 0 … n−1, so the ground state has L = 0 — a circular orbit is impossible.
Worked example: the Hα line ✍ Balmer α is the transition n = 3 → n = 2:
ΔE = 13.6(1/2² − 1/3²) = 13.6 × 5/36 = 1.89 eV → λ = 1240/1.89 = 656 nm, hydrogen's red line. Ionising the ground state needs exactly 13.6 eV.
Schrödinger's hydrogen The energies are the same, Eₙ = −13.6/n² eV, but the orbital quantum number l = 0 … n−1 and m_l = −l … +l, so the n-th shell holds n² states: 1s · 2s, 2p · 3s, 3p, 3d …
1/λ = R(1/n₁² − 1/n₂²)

8Expectation Values, Uncertainty & Correspondence

written favourite
Expectation value An observable A has no definite value in a state Ψ; it has only an average over many identically prepared copies: ⟨A⟩ = ∫Ψ* Â Ψ dτ. For Ψ = ΣciΨi in an orthonormal set, ⟨A⟩ = Σ|ci|²⟨Ai⟩i.
QuantityExpectation valueSpread (standard deviation)
position⟨x⟩ = ∫Ψ* x Ψ dτΔx = √(⟨x²⟩ − ⟨x⟩²)
momentum⟨p⟩ = ∫Ψ* (−iħ∇)Ψ dτΔp = √(⟨p²⟩ − ⟨p⟩²)
energy⟨E⟩ = ∫Ψ* ĤΨ dτΔE (0 in a stationary state)
Heisenberg uncertainty principle (1927) Δx · Δp ≥ ħ/2
Equality only for a minimum-uncertainty Gaussian packet. x̂ and p̂ do not commute ([x̂, p̂] = iħ), so no state can be an eigenstate of both.

Correspondence principle (Bohr, 1920)

SystemQuantumLarge-n limit
boxEn = n²h²/8mL²ΔE/E = (2n+1)/n² → 0
oscillatorEn = (n+½)ħωconstant ħω → smooth
Bohr H atomL = nħ, r = n²a₀L/ħ → ∞, orbit looks classical
Memory trick "You may have it small or small — never both." Δx is a length, Δp a momentum; their product is an action (J·s). Put ħ = 0 and you get the classical world back.
Exam trap The sign is ≥, and equality is the exception. For the box ground state ΔxΔp ≈ 0.57 ħ, not ħ/2.
Worked example: electron in the 0.1 nm box ✍ ⟨x⟩ = L/2 and ⟨x²⟩ = L²(1/3 − 1/2π²) = 0.2827 L², so Δx = √(0.2827 − 0.25)L = 0.181L = 1.81 × 10⁻¹¹ m. Then Δp ≥ ħ/(2Δx) = 1.0546 × 10⁻³⁴/(3.62 × 10⁻¹¹) = 2.9 × 10⁻²⁴ kg·m/s, i.e. Δv ≥ 3.2 × 10⁶ m/s — while the particle's own speed is only √(2E₁/m) = 3.6 × 10⁶ m/s. Position and momentum cannot both be pinned down.
