1Electromagnetic Waves: Two Fields, One Wave

high-yield
Definition An EM wave is a travelling coupled oscillation of an electric field E and a magnetic field B. The two fields are perpendicular to each other and both perpendicular to the direction of travel. Because a changing E field creates a B field and vice versa, the wave needs no material medium.
Three lines to memorise  c = 1/√(μ₀ε₀) = 3 × 10⁸ m/s  ·  c = fλ  ·  in vacuum E₀/B₀ = c, and E and B are in phase (peaks together)

E and B reach their peaks at the same instant — that is what "in phase" means.

  • Origin: Maxwell predicted EM waves (1864–65); Hertz generated and detected them (1887).
  • Nature: transverse and uncharged, so an EM wave is not deflected by an electric or magnetic field — yet it still exerts forces on charges.
  • One speed for all: every EM wave travels at c in vacuum; only f and λ change from band to band.
  • Energy flux (Poynting vector) S = E₀B₀/μ₀, directed along the propagation. Light itself is an EM wave (Maxwell, 1865).

Memory trick "E–B–c right": E, B and the travel direction are mutually at right angles, and the wave travels along E × B.
Exam trap "EM waves are deflected by magnetic fields" is false — they carry no charge. But they do exert forces on charges, so the two statements are not the same.
Worked example ✍ A station transmits on 90 MHz: λ = 3 × 10⁸ / 90 × 10⁶ = 3.33 m → a radio wave; on 900 MHz, λ = 0.33 m → a microwave. Photon energy there: 3.7 × 10⁻⁷ eV.

3Speed in a Medium, Refractive Index, Polarisation

MCQ + Written
In a material medium The wave drags the medium's charges along, so it slows down: v = 1/√(με) = c/√(μrεr). The refractive index n = c/v = √(μrεr); for glass and water μr ≈ 1, so n = √εr.

λ changes in a medium. ν (frequency) never does — check every question for this.

Refraction
Snell's law: sin i / sin r = v₁/v₂ = n₂/n₁ · entering a denser medium bends towards the normal, so r < i.

Photon energy hf never changes either, so the speed drops because λ shrinks: λ′ = λ/n.

Polarisation in detail

Polarisation = restricting the vibration of E to one plane across the direction of travel. The plane of polarisation is the plane containing both the direction of travel and the electric vector. Malus's law: I = I₀ cos²θ after a second filter at angle θ; unpolarised light through one ideal Polaroid drops to half the intensity. Brewster's law: tan ip = n₂/n₁ — at that angle ip + r = 90°, the reflected ray is perpendicular to the refracted ray, and the reflected light is completely plane-polarised with E perpendicular to the plane of incidence. Uses: sunglasses, camera filters, LCD panels, photoelastic stress analysis.

Memory trick "Enter, bend, stay": light enters a denser medium, bends toward the normal, but its frequency stays. Only λ shrinks, λ′ = λ/n.
Exam trap The commonest error is claiming the frequency changes when light enters glass or water. It does not. And sound cannot be polarised — it is longitudinal.
Worked example ✍ 600 nm light in air enters glass of n = 1.5. (i) f = c/λ = 5 × 10¹⁴ Hz (unchanged). (ii) λ′ = 600/1.5 = 400 nm. (iii) v = c/1.5 = 2 × 10⁸ m/s. (iv) A medium with μr = 4, εr = 9 → n = √36 = 6, so v = c/6 = 5 × 10⁷ m/s.

4Black-Body Radiation & Planck's Quantum

high-yield
Definition A black body is an ideal body that absorbs all EM radiation falling on it at every wavelength and reflects nothing. Kirchhoff's law: a good absorber is a good emitter, so at thermal equilibrium the emitted spectrum is the absorption spectrum.
LawFormulaConstant
Stefan–BoltzmannP/A = σT⁴σ = 5.67 × 10⁻⁸ W m⁻² K⁻⁴

Planck's radiation laweλdλ = 8πhc dλ / λ⁵(e^{hc/λkT} − 1)k = 1.38 × 10⁻²³ J/K
Planck, 1900 Energy is exchanged in discrete packets hf, not continuously: a simple oscillator may only hold n·hf. That single assumption removed the ultraviolet catastrophe, in which classical theory demanded infinite energy at short wavelengths. As T rises, the peak slides IR → red → orange → yellow → white; the Sun's surface (≈ 5800 K) looks slightly bluish-white.
Memory trick "Wien finds the peak, Stefan counts the heat": Wien (λmaxT = const) gives T from one wavelength; Stefan–Boltzmann (σT⁴) gives total power.
Exam trap Wien's law finds the peak wavelength, not the total energy. Planck proposed quanta for black-body radiation in 1900; Einstein (1905) pushed them into light as photons.
Worked examples ✍ (i) λmax = 500 nm → T = 2.9 × 10⁻³ / 5 × 10⁻⁷ = 5800 K. (ii) A room at 300 K → λmax = 9.7 μm, deep infrared. (iii) At 1000 K: P/A = 5.67 × 10⁻⁸ × 10¹² = 5.67 × 10⁴ W m⁻². (iv) ν = 6 × 10¹⁴ Hz → E = hf = 3.98 × 10⁻¹⁹ J = 2.48 eV.

5The Photoelectric Effect

written favourite
Definition Emission of electrons from a metal surface when EM radiation of frequency above the threshold falls on it. Hertz observed it (1887), Lenard studied it (1902), Einstein 1905, Nobel Prize 1921.
Einstein's equation  hf = φ + Kmax; φ = work function = h f₀; f₀ = threshold frequency; λ₀ = hc/φ; stopping potential: eV₀ = Kmax.
You change…PhotocurrentKmax, V₀
Intensity ↑, f fixedincreasesno change
f ↑, intensity fixed≈ no changeincreases linearly
λ ↑ but still < λ₀no changedecreases
f < f₀, any intensityno emission at all, however bright · emission is instantaneous
Memory trick "Brightness counts, energy ranks": intensity decides how many electrons escape; frequency decides how fast they escape.
Exam trap Doubling the intensity does not double Kmax. The Kmax vs f graph is a straight line of slope h through (f₀, 0).
  • Why wave theory fails: no emission below f₀ however intense the light; Kmax depends on f, not intensity; emission is instantaneous.
  • Photon picture: one photon of energy hf is absorbed by one electron, so hf = φ + Kmax.
Worked examples ✍ (i) φ = 2.0 eV, λ = 400 nm → E = 1240/400 = 3.1 eV → Kmax = 3.1 − 2.0 = 1.1 eV → V₀ = 1.1 V. (ii) λ = 500 nm gives V₀ = 1.48 V → φ = 2.48 − 1.48 = 1.0 eV, f₀ = 6 × 10¹⁴ Hz, λ₀ = 500 nm.
IT link Photodiodes in optical-fibre receivers, CCD / CMOS image sensors and solar cells all detect single photons — the same hf = φ + Kmax relation, read out as a current.

6Compton Effect, Pair Production, Photons

high-yield
Photon Light in packets: energy E = hf = hc/λ, momentum p = h/λ = E/c, rest mass = 0, charge 0, spin 0, speed always c. Because m = 0 while v = c, a photon's momentum and energy are always locked together by p = E/c.

Δλ is a property of the electron it hit, not of the photon: the same shift for any wavelength.

Compton effect An X-ray photon scattered by a nearly free electron comes out with a longer wavelength. A.H. Compton (1923, Nobel 1927) — direct proof that photons carry momentum.
Shift formula Δλ = λ′ − λ = (h/mec)(1 − cos φ), with the Compton wavelength λC = h/mec = 2.43 pm
φ0°60°90°180°
Δλ01.21 pm2.43 pm4.85 pm (max)

The shift depends only on φ — not on the incident λ, not on the target material. The energy the photon loses becomes kinetic energy of the recoiling electron, so energy is still conserved.

Pair production & annihilation

  • Pair production: a photon near a nucleus converts into an electron–positron pair, γ → e⁻ + e⁺. Energy must reach 2mec² = 1.022 MeV, so λ ≤ 1.21 × 10⁻³ nm; the nucleus is needed to absorb the recoil momentum.
  • Annihilation: e⁻ + e⁺ → γ + γ, and each photon carries at least 0.511 MeV. Two photons, never one — a single photon would break momentum conservation.
  • Photon count rises and energy per photon falls: mass is converted into radiation.
Memory trick "0.511 each, 1.022 together": one electron's rest energy is 0.511 MeV; a photon must bring twice that to make a pair.
Exam trap The shift is independent of wavelength and material — visible light would shift too, but by only ~2 pm out of 500 nm, which is undetectable. That is why the effect was found with X-rays.
Worked examples ✍ (i) A 60 keV photon (λ = 0.0207 nm) scattered at 60°: Δλ = 2.43 × (1 − cos 60°) = 1.21 pm, so λ′ = 0.0219 nm. (ii) Longest wavelength that can create a pair: λ = hc/E = 1240 / 1.022 × 10⁶ = 1.21 × 10⁻³ nm. (iii) At 180° the maximum shift is 2λC = 4.85 pm.

7de Broglie Matter Waves & Wave-Particle Duality

high-yield
Hypothesis, 1924 If light behaves like a particle, then a particle must behave like a wave. Louis de Broglie attached a wavelength to every moving particle: λ = h/p = h/mv = h/√(2mK). Heavier or faster ⇒ shorter λ, so macroscopic objects have λ far too tiny to observe.
  • Davisson–Germer (1927): electrons at 54 V scattered from a nickel crystal gave a clear diffraction pattern. λ = 1.226/√54 = 0.167 nm — the same order as the Ni lattice spacing, 0.215 nm — so matter waves were confirmed.
  • G. P. Thomson (1927) sent electrons through a thin polycrystalline foil and got concentric rings, exactly like the optical diffraction rings of light through a small aperture.
  • Uses: the electron microscope (short λ ⇒ tiny resolution), electron diffraction in a TEM, and LEED for reading chip surfaces.

Bohr orbits as standing waves

Only an integer number of de Broglie wavelengths fits round a stable orbit, 2πr = nλ. Putting λ = h/p = h/mv gives mvr = nh/2π = nħ, Bohr's quantised angular momentum — now derived from wave physics. Phase velocity, group velocity, the wave function and the Schrödinger equation are taken further in the next lesson of this chapter; λ = h/p is all that is needed here.

Memory trick "Light → particle (photoelectric, Compton, pair). Particle → wave (de Broglie, Davisson–Germer). The same h sits in the middle of both halves.
Exam trap Matter waves are not ripples of a medium. A photon has zero rest mass, an electron does not. Saying "light is a wave and a particle at once" is wrong.
Worked example ✍ (i) 54 V: λ = 1.226/√54 = 0.167 nm, comparable with the Ni spacing 0.215 nm, so diffraction is visible. (ii) A neutron of mass 1.675 × 10⁻²⁷ kg at 1000 m/s has p = 1.675 × 10⁻²⁴ kg·m/s, so λ = 3.96 × 10⁻¹⁰ m = 0.396 nm.

written favourite

Memory trick Only the series ending at n = 2 (Balmer) is visible; n = 1 gives UV, n ≥ 3 gives IR.
Exam trap Bohr's model reproduces hydrogen only — it fails for helium and heavier atoms. En is negative; its magnitude shrinks as n grows.
Worked examples ✍ (i) E₂ = −13.6/4 = −3.4 eV; ionising from n = 2 needs 3.4 eV. (ii) n = 3 → 2: ΔE = 13.6(1/4 − 1/9) = 1.89 eV → λ = 656 nm, the red Hα line. (iii) Radius of n = 3: 0.476 nm. (iv) Excitation n = 1 → 3 needs 12.09 eV.
