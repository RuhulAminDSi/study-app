AI · IntroductionDefinition & Turing Test

1What Is AI? Definition & the Turing Test

highest-yield ★
Definition Artificial Intelligence is the branch of computer science that builds systems able to perform tasks which normally require human intelligence — reasoning, learning, perception, planning. The test is behavioural: can the system do the task?, never "does it understand?". The nesting used in almost every paper is AI ⊃ machine learning ⊃ deep learning.
The Turing Test, 1950 Turing set the imitation game in Computing Machinery and Intelligence: an interrogator holds a written conversation with a hidden human and a hidden machine, one reply stream at a time. The machine passes if the interrogator cannot reliably tell which one is the machine. Conditions: natural language, no prior information, a third human as a safety valve.
Exam trap Passing proves indistinguishability of behaviour, never consciousness. ELIZA (Weizenbaum, 1966) fooled many users while understanding nothing; Chinese room: Searle, 1980.
CapabilityWhat it must doExampleCapabilityWhat it must doExample
Reasoningsolve new problemschess engine, A*Languageread and write texttranslator
Knowledgestore factssemantic networkPerceptionread the sensorsspeech-to-text
Learningimprove from dataclassifierPlanningpick actions in orderroute planner
Memory trick "Turing tested the output, not the brain." Nothing in the test asks the machine to report what it is doing inside — only whether a human can sort the replies into "person" and "machine".
Worked example ✍ is each system AI? A calculator evaluating 2 + 2 is not AI: no inference, no adaptation. A rule-based system returning an infection and a drug from symptoms is AI — it reasons over stored knowledge to produce something the user did not supply. A CNN labelling chest X-rays is AI and specifically machine learning.

2How AI Began: History & the Two Winters

frequently tested
Definition An AI winter is a period in which funding, publications and interest collapse because promised results failed to arrive. AI has had two: roughly 1974–1980 and 1987–1993. The pattern repeats — over-promise, under-deliver, funding stops.

Exam trap Three dates get confused: 1950 is the Turing test, 1955 is when McCarthy coined the term, and 1956 is the workshop at which the field was born.
Memory trick "Test, name, birth": 1950 test → 1955 name → 1956 birth. A winter starts when a report appears — Lighthill 1974, ALPAC 1983.
Worked example ✍ which claim explains which winter?
  1. "The Lighthill report said AI had delivered none of its promises, so funding stopped." → winter 1, 1974–1980.
  2. "Companies bought Lisp machines whose expert systems were too slow and too narrow." → winter 2, 1987–1993.
  3. "A single-layer perceptron cannot learn XOR." → not a winter but a technical limit, exposed in 1969.

AI · IntroductionSymbolic AI

3Old AI: Symbolic / Rule-Based AI

classical, 1950s–80s
Definition Symbolic AI — also classical, rule-based or knowledge-based AI — represents intelligence as explicit symbols and rules that a program manipulates. Knowledge is written down by people; inference is a formal procedure: deduction, search, or rule chaining.
FamilyCore ideaRepresentative systemFamilyCore ideaRepresentative system
Expert systemsIF–THEN rules + inference engineMYCIN, DENDRAL, XCONKnowledge rep.facts as graphs, frames, rulessemantic network, production rules
Searchexplore a state space with a heuristicBFS, DFS, A* (1968)Planningbuild an action sequence from a goalSTRIPS (1971)
Logicdeduction over predicatespropositional / first-order logicBelief revisionrevise beliefs when facts changetruth-maintenance systems
Exam trap An expert system never learns: every rule was written in advance, so adding one can silently break another — the knowledge-acquisition problem. Forward chaining is data-driven (fire rules as facts arrive); backward chaining is goal-driven (ask what must be true to prove the goal). STRIPS is planning, not logic.
Worked example ✍ MYCIN, both directions MYCIN (Stanford, early 1970s) held a few hundred IF–THEN rules on bacterial infections and drug choice. Forward: the patient has a fever, so fire every rule whose IF matches — conclusions arrive from the data. Backward: the goal is "recommend ampicillin"; ask which conditions must hold, then look for each one. Its advice was rated as acceptable as a specialist's, yet it was never used routinely: maintaining the rule base cost more than the consultations saved.
Memory trick "Symbols are written by people, so the bottleneck is human." No rule, no knowledge — and no way to discover a rule the expert never told you.
The one clear commercial success XCON (DEC, 1980) configured VAX systems from a customer order; reports put its savings at tens of millions of dollars a year. Narrow, repetitive, rule-shaped work is what symbolic AI is good at.

AI · IntroductionNeural networks

4Connectionist AI: From the Neuron to Deep Learning

modern core ★
Definition Connectionist AI — sub-symbolic, neural or machine-learning AI — builds intelligence out of many simple units wired in layers. Each unit computes a weighted sum of its inputs and passes it through an activation; the knowledge lives not in written rules but in the weights, which are fitted to data by an optimisation algorithm.
YearMilestoneWhat it addedYearMilestoneWhat it added
1943McCulloch–Pitts neuronlogic gates as a neuron, no learning1989LeNet / CNNone filter shared across an image
1958Rosenblatt's perceptronfirst network trained from data1997LSTMa cell that remembers across sequences
1969Minsky & Papertone layer cannot represent XOR2014GANtwo networks compete to generate
1986backpropagationtrain many layers, credit layer by layer2017Transformerattention only; basis of language models
Exam trap A single-layer perceptron can learn AND, OR, NOT but not XOR — XOR is not linearly separable. That 1969 result was never a proof that networks cannot work. And deep = many hidden layers, not many neurons in one layer.
Worked example ✍ one threshold unit, then two layers A unit computes y = 1 if Σwᵢxᵢ ≥ θ. For AND take w = (1, 1), θ = 1.5 → only (1,1) passes; for OR take θ = 0.5. For XOR a single unit cannot do it, because one straight line cannot separate (0,1),(1,0) from (0,0),(1,1). With two layers it is trivial: hidden units computing OR and AND, then an output unit weighting them (1, 1) with threshold 1 — (0,1) and (1,1) give 1, the rest give 0 ✓.
Memory trick "Symbolic AI is a laws book; connectionist AI is a trained reflex." Laws are written and argued with; reflexes are fitted and used without explanation.
Which architecture for which data CNN for images · RNN/LSTM for sequences read in order · Transformer when long-range attention matters, which is what language models use · the price of all of them is data and compute, paid for in interpretability.

AI · IntroductionEvolutionary & probabilistic

5Evolutionary and Probabilistic AI

third & fourth families
Definition Two more families complete the picture. Evolutionary AI keeps a population of candidate solutions and breeds the fitter ones — selection, crossover, mutation — exactly as natural selection does. Probabilistic AI refuses to be certain: it keeps a distribution over possibilities and updates it with Bayes' rule as evidence arrives.
Bayes only says "most probable" — not true.
FamilyWhere the knowledge sitsRepresentative methodsFails when
Symbolicin rules written by humansexpert systems, A*, resolution, STRIPSthe rules are missing or wrong
Connectionistin weights fitted to dataperceptron, CNN, RNN, Transformerdata is thin, or an explanation is required
Evolutionaryin the fittest of a populationgenetic algorithm, genetic programming, ant colonyevaluation is slow, or the space is huge
Probabilisticin conditional probability tablesBayesian network, HMM, Naive Bayesthe independence assumptions break
Memory trick "GA is guided random: probability does the mutating, fitness does the choosing." Bayes asks which explanation the evidence makes most likely, not which explanation is true.
Exam trap A genetic algorithm is not random guessing: selection is directed and the best candidates are carried unchanged (elitism). In a Bayesian network arrows run from cause to effect and every node carries a conditional probability table; an HMM has hidden states plus emissions and powers speech recognition.
Worked example ✍ one generation, and one Bayes base rate
  1. GA: population {(3, 7), (5, 2), (2, 4)} maximising xy. Fitness: 21, 10, 8. Keep (3,7), copy it twice, mutate the copies slightly → {(3,7), (3,6), (4,8)}. The best product rises from 21 to 32 in one generation — the search is improving, not restarting.
  2. Bayes: a disease hits 1 % of people; a test finds it in 90 % of the sick and wrongly flags 10 % of the healthy. Of 10 000 people, 100 are sick and 90 test positive; of the 9 900 healthy, 990 test positive. So P(sick | positive) = 90/(90 + 990) = 1/12 ≈ 8.3 % — the base rate dominates, which is why "the test is 90 % accurate" does not mean a positive result means 90 %.

6Old vs New AI Techniques, Compared

the classic comparison
Definition "Old AI" is the symbolic, rule-based work of the 1950s–1980s; "new AI" is the statistical, data-driven work that took over from the 1990s onward. The dividing line is not "old = wrong, new = right" — it is where the knowledge comes from: a person wrote it versus data supplied it.
FeatureOld AINew AIFeatureOld AINew AI
Approachsymbolic, rule-baseddata-driven, statisticalData needednone, if rules completelarge, often labelled
Knowledge froman expert writes ruleslearned from dataInterpretabilityhigh — rule shownlow — "black box"
Reasoningdeduction, searchgeneralisationScalabilitypoor — rules collidegood — data helps
Uncertaintybinary true / falseprobabilitiesComputesmall machinesGPUs / TPUs
Adaptabilitylow — means rewritinghigh — retrainSystemsMYCIN, STRIPS, A*k-means, CNN, LLM
Memory trick "Old AI is brittle and honest: it breaks on anything unanticipated but always shows its work. New AI is flexible and opaque: it handles anything in distribution but cannot say why."
Common misconception "Symbolic AI is obsolete" is false: SAT and theorem provers, planners, compilers and test-case generators are symbolic systems working today. What changed is the default for perception and language, not the existence of reasoning.
Exam trap New AI is not "AI plus data" — knowledge representation and search are still AI whether the symbols were typed by a person or extracted from data. Interpretability is a requirement, not a luxury: in medicine, credit and public administration a system that cannot justify a decision is not deployable at all.
Worked example ✍ choose the approach Admit patients whose lab values match eleven published rules → symbolic expert system: the rules exist and are auditable. Flag tumours in a new hospital's scans with no labels yet → deep learning: there is nothing to write rules from, and images are what CNNs handle. Choose routes for 500 vans from last year's traffic data → learned prediction: the answer is a number nobody can write a rule for.

AI · IntroductionStrong vs weak AI

7Strong AI vs Weak AI

viva favourite ★
Definition Weak AI (narrow AI, ANI) is built for one specific task and displays intelligence inside that task and nowhere else — a chess engine, a spam filter, a face unlock. Strong AI (general AI, AGI) would match humans across every cognitive task, learn any task from few examples, and form its own goals. Superintelligence (ASI) would exceed the best human performance on everything.
PropertyWeak AI (all that exists today)Strong AI (the goal)
Scopeone task, narrow domainany task a human can do
Transfer between tasksnothing carries overone skill supports the next
Training datamillions of examples for that taska few examples, or none
Own goalsnone — objectives sit in codeforms, revises, pursues its own
Consciousnessnot required, not claimedusually assumed, still undefined
Exists?yes — every AI product on saleno, never demonstrated
Exam trap Winning at chess or Go is not strong AI: Deep Blue (1997) and AlphaGo (2016, 4–1 over Lee Sedol) beat world champions at one game and were helpless at everything else. The Chinese-room argument (Searle, 1980) says running the right symbols is not understanding them.
Where the real debate sits Not on whether machines can beat humans at games — that is settled. It sits on whether understanding can exist without consciousness, and on aligning an objective with human intent.
Memory trick "Weak is wide but shallow across data; strong is deep across tasks." Today AI has breadth without understanding — a million narrow skills, no mind behind them.
Worked example ✍ classify four systems A chess engine → weak AI: superb at chess, unable to explain a photograph. A hospital triage rule base → weak AI: one narrow task, but it can state which rule fired. A large language model that writes code and translates → weak AI: many tasks, yet it needs a new fine-tune for a new domain. A system that learns one task, carries the skill into a second and sets its own goals → the only strong AI candidate, and none exists.

8The Agent View: Rational Agents & PEAS

written favourite ★
Definition An agent is anything that perceives its environment through sensors and acts on it through actuators. An agent function maps the whole history of percepts to the next action. A rational agent takes the action best for its goals — best given what it knows, not best in reality. A goal-oriented system is specified with PEAS: Performance, Environment, Actuators, Sensors.

Learning agentimproves its own decision rulecan learn the wrong thingExample: vacuumsuck if dirty, else move rightneeds memory to stop looping
AgentEnvironmentActuatorsSensors
vacuumtwo dirty roomsmove, suckposition, dirt
droneairspace, partly observablerotors, dropGPS, IMU
spam filterone mailboxspam / inboxheaders, body
Exam trap Rational does not mean human-like, and an agent need not have a body — a spam filter is an agent. A simple reflex agent with no memory can never leave a loop, and a utility-based agent differs from a goal-based one by comparing several goals at once.
Worked example ✍ the vacuum agent Simple reflex: dirt here → suck, else move right. In a two-room world it walks back and forth through the dirt it just cleaned — an infinite loop, having no memory of where it has been. Model-based reflex: record which squares are clean — two extra states, no search, loop gone. Goal-based: plan the move sequence first. Utility-based: add cleaning time and battery to the score.
IT link A monitoring or autoscaling system gets the same four-part description in its design document: what good looks like, where it runs, what it can change, what it can read.

9Current Techniques: ML, Deep Learning, NLP

the working stack
Definition "New AI" is not one technique but a stack. Machine learning is the base: systems that fit parameters to data instead of being handed rules. Deep learning is machine learning with many layers. Probabilistic reasoning handles uncertainty, evolutionary computation searches, and NLP is the application area that drove the last decade.
ParadigmFeedbackStandard algorithmsTypical task
Supervisedlabelled output per inputlinear & logistic regression, decision tree, SVM, k-NNclassification, regression
Unsupervisednone — structure discoveredk-means, hierarchical, DBSCAN, PCA, Aprioriclustering, association
Reinforcementa scalar reward after actingQ-learning, SARSA, policy gradientgames, control, scheduling
LayerWhat it contributes
Deep learningCNN for images, RNN/LSTM for sequences, Transformer for language models
ProbabilisticBayes' rule, Bayesian networks, HMM, Gaussian models
Evolutionarygenetic algorithms, genetic programming, annealing, ant colony
NLPtokenisation, stemming, lemmatisation, POS tagging, NER, translation
Memory trick "A teacher gives answers (supervised) · no teacher, find groups (unsupervised) · a critic gives only a score (reinforcement)."
Exam trap Supervised learning requires labelled outputs; clustering without labels is unsupervised, and reward after an action is reinforcement. Stemming chops suffixes mechanically ("studies" → "studi"); lemmatisation uses the dictionary ("studies" → "study").
Worked example ✍ label each scenario
  1. Labelled e-mails marked spam / not spam → supervised classification.
  2. Grouping shopkeepers by basket similarity, no labels → unsupervised clustering.
  3. A rover rewarded for distance without falling → reinforcement learning.
  4. Finding "diaper ⇒ beer" rules from baskets → unsupervised association rules (Apriori).
  5. Estimating the chance a loan defaults → supervised; a 0–1 probability is logistic regression.
  6. Predicting house prices from size, location, age → supervised regression; least squares minimises the sum of squared residuals.
