1Capacitor & Inductor: the Storage Elements

high-yield
Capacitor Two conductors separated by a dielectric. Stores energy in the electric field. C = Q/V, unit farad (1 F = 1 C/V). Parallel plates: C = εA/d.
Inductor A coil of wire. Stores energy in the magnetic field. L = NΦ/I, unit henry (1 H = 1 Wb/A = 1 V·s/A). Solenoid: L = μN²A/l.

Memory trick L behaves like R (series adds). C is R upside-down (parallel adds). C and L are duals: swap v ↔ i and C ↔ L to turn one row into the other.
Worked example ✍ 2 μF, 3 μF, 6 μF on 100 V. Series: 1/C = ½ + ⅓ + ⅙ = 1 → C = 1 μF; Q = CV = 100 μC on each; V = Q/C → 50 V, 33.3 V, 16.7 V (sum 100 ✓); W = ½ × 1 μ × 100² = 5 mJ. Parallel: C = 11 μF, W = 55 mJ.
Inductors 4 H ∥ 12 H (no mutual coupling) = 48/16 = 3 H; at 2 A, W = ½ × 3 × 2² = 6 J.
Exam trap Capacitors in series carry the same charge, so the smallest C takes the largest voltage (2 μF gets 50 V). Series C is always smaller than the smallest one. Ideal C and L dissipate no power: they store energy and give it back.

2RC & RL Transients (First Order)

written favourite
Transient The change from one steady state to another after a switch operates. In a circuit with one C or one L it is an exponential set by the time constant τ: τ = RC  or  τ = L/R (R = Thevenin resistance seen by C or L).
One formula for every case: x(t) = x(∞) + [x(0⁺) − x(∞)] e−t/τ (x = vC or iL)
tchargingdecay left
1τ63.2%36.8%
2τ86.5%13.5%
3τ95.0%5.0%
4τ98.2%1.8%
5τ99.3%0.7%

RC charge: vC = V(1 − e−t/RC)

RC discharge: vC = V₀e−t/RC

RL rise: i = (V/R)(1 − e−Rt/L)

RL decay: i = I₀e−Rt/L

Worked example: RC ✍ 10 kΩ, 100 μF, 12 V: τ = 1 s; i(0⁺) = 1.2 mA; vC(1 s) = 12 × 0.632 = 7.59 V; 6 V at t = τ ln 2 = 0.693 s
Worked example: RL ✍ 2 H, 4 Ω, 12 V: τ = L/R = 0.5 s; i(∞) = 3 A; i(0.5 s) = 3 × 0.632 = 1.90 A; final energy ½ × 2 × 3² = 9 J
Memory trick 63 – 86 – 95 – 98 – 99: one number per τ. "Five taus and it's done."
Exam trap RL: τ = L/R, not LR. A bigger R makes RL faster but RC slower. iC and vL decay (e−t/τ).

3Second-Order (RLC) Response

ω₀ = 1/√(LC) (undamped natural frequency) · damping factor α = R/2L (series), α = 1/(2RC) (parallel) · ζ = α/ω₀ · damped frequency ωd = √(ω₀² − α²)

ConditionResponseSeries RLC
α > ω₀ (ζ > 1)overdamped: two exponentials, slowR > 2√(L/C)
α = ω₀ (ζ = 1)critically damped: fastest without oscillationR = 2√(L/C)
α < ω₀ (ζ < 1)underdamped: decaying oscillation at ωdR < 2√(L/C)

e.g. L = 1 H, C = 1 μF → ω₀ = 1000 rad/s; critical R (series) = 2√(10⁶) = 2 kΩ.

4Sinusoids: Peak, RMS, Average

high-yield
Sinusoid v(t) = Vm sin(ωt + φ): Vm = amplitude (peak), ω = 2πf rad/s, T = 1/f, φ = phase angle. Bangladesh mains: 220 V rms, 50 Hz.
RMS The DC value that gives the same heating in a resistor: Vrms = √(mean of v²). Meters and ratings ("220 V") are RMS.
Average Mean over a half cycle: 2Vm/π. Over a full cycle a pure sine averages 0.

Worked example 1 ✍ BD mains 220 V, 50 Hz: Vm = 220√2 = 311 V; Vp-p = 622 V; ω = 2π × 50 = 314 rad/s; T = 20 ms; Vavg = 0.637 × 311 = 198 V.
Worked example 2 ✍ v = 100 sin(314t + 30°) V: Vm = 100 V, Vrms = 70.7 V, f = 314/2π ≈ 50 Hz, φ = +30°; at t = 0, v = 100 sin 30° = 50 V.

Phase difference

v = 10 sin(ωt + 20°), i = 2 sin(ωt − 40°) → v leads i by 20° − (−40°) = 60°. Write both as sin with positive amplitude first: cos ωt = sin(ωt + 90°), −sin ωt = sin(ωt + 180°).

Derivation (written) ✍ Vrms² = (1/T)∫0T Vm² sin²ωt dt = (Vm²/T)∫0T ½(1 − cos 2ωt) dt = Vm²/2 → Vrms = Vm/√2. Half-cycle average: (1/π)∫0π Vm sin θ dθ = 2Vm/π.
Memory trick The sine's four numbers: 0.707 · 0.637 · 1.11 · 1.414 (rms, average, form, crest).
Exam trap "220 V" is RMS; insulation must withstand the peak 311 V. Full-wave rectified sine has the same rms and average as a sine.

5Phasors & Complex Numbers

high-yield
Phasor A complex number that carries the magnitude and phase of a sinusoid of a known frequency: v = Vm sin(ωt + φ) → V = V∠φ (V usually rms). Think of a vector rotating anticlockwise at ω; its vertical projection traces the sine. It turns calculus (d/dt → jω) into algebra.
Rectangular ↔ polar
a + jb = r∠θ,  r = √(a² + b²), θ = tan⁻¹(b/a)
a = r cos θ,  b = r sin θ
j = √−1 = 1∠90°, j² = −1, 1/j = −j
Add/subtract in rectangular; multiply/divide in polar (multiply r, add angles)
Worked example 1 ✍ I = 100∠0° / (3 + j4) = 100∠0° / 5∠53.13° = 20∠−53.13° A = 12 − j16 A
Worked example 2 ✍ 10∠0° + 10∠90° = 10 + j10 = 14.14∠45°, not 20

Rv = RiV = RIRin phase (0°)

Ci = C dv/dtV = I/(jωC)−jXC = 1/(jωC)I leads by 90°
Memory trick ELI the ICE man: in L, E (voltage) comes before I; in C, I comes before E. Or CIVIL: in C, I before V; V before I in L.
Exam trap Phasors only combine sinusoids of the same frequency. Adding peak values of out-of-phase waves (10 + 10 = 20) is wrong: add them as complex numbers.

6Impedance, Admittance & AC Networks

MCQ + Written
Impedance Z = V/I = R + jX = |Z|∠θ (Ω). R = resistance, X = reactance. |Z| = √(R² + X²), θ = tan⁻¹(X/R). X > 0 inductive, X < 0 capacitive.
Admittance Y = 1/Z = G + jB, unit siemens (S). G = conductance, B = susceptance. G = R/(R² + X²), B = −X/(R² + X²).
InductorCapacitor
ReactanceXL = 2πfLXC = 1/(2πfC)

Network rules (same as DC, with Z) Series: Z = Z₁ + Z₂ + … · Parallel: Y = Y₁ + Y₂ + …, two branches Z = Z₁Z₂/(Z₁ + Z₂)
Voltage divider: V₁ = V · Z₁/(Z₁ + Z₂) · Current divider: I₁ = I · Z₂/(Z₁ + Z₂) = I · Y₁/(Y₁ + Y₂)
Worked example 1: reactance ✍ 0.1 H at 50 Hz: XL = 2π × 50 × 0.1 = 31.4 Ω. 100 μF at 50 Hz: XC = 1/(2π × 50 × 10⁻⁴) = 31.8 Ω.
Worked example 2: parallel ✍ R = 10 Ω ∥ XC = 10 Ω on 100∠0° V. Y = 0.1 + j0.1 = 0.1414∠45° S; Z = 1/Y = 7.07∠−45° = 5 − j5 Ω; I = VY = 14.14∠45° A (leading).
Worked example 3: divider ✍ 100∠0° V across R = 6 Ω in series with jXL = j8 Ω. Z = 10∠53.13°. VL = 100 × j8/(6 + j8) = 80∠36.87° V, VR = 60∠−53.13° V. Check: 60 + 80 = 140 ≠ 100, but √(60² + 80²) = 100 ✓.
Exam trap G = 1/R only if the branch has no reactance; for R + jX, G = R/(R² + X²). Voltage magnitudes in AC do not add arithmetically.
Lesson 2 link AC mesh and nodal analysis, Thevenin, Norton and superposition work exactly as in Lesson 2 (Nodal, Mesh & Circuit Theorems), using complex Z. Balanced Δ → Y: ZY = ZΔ/3. AC maximum power transfer: ZL = Zth* (complex conjugate), Pmax = |Vth|²/(4Rth).

Circuits · C, L & ACSeries & parallel RLC

7Series RLC Circuit

written favourite
Formulas Z = R + j(XL − XC) · |Z| = √(R² + (XL − XC)²) · φ = tan⁻¹((XL − XC)/R) · V = √(VR² + (VL − VC)²) · pf = cos φ = R/|Z|
XL > XC: inductive, I lags · XL < XC: capacitive, I leads · XL = XC: resonance, Z = R
Worked example ✍ R = 8 Ω, XL = 10 Ω, XC = 4 Ω on 100 V rms.
  1. X = 10 − 4 = 6 Ω (inductive); Z = √(8² + 6²) = 10 Ω, φ = tan⁻¹(6/8) = 36.87°
  2. I = 100/10 = 10 A, lagging; VR = 80 V, VL = 100 V, VC = 40 V; check √(80² + 60²) = 100 ✓
  3. pf = 8/10 = 0.8 lagging; P = I²R = 800 W, Q = I²X = 600 VAR, S = VI = 1000 VA
Exam trap In series RLC the voltage across L or C can equal or exceed the supply (here VL = 100 V = V). Only the phasor sum must equal V.

8Parallel RLC Circuit

Y = 1/R + j(ωC − 1/ωL) · I = V·Y

I = √(IR² + (IC − IL)²)

IL > IC (XL < XC): inductive, lagging

IC > IL: capacitive, leading

Worked example ✍ R = 20 Ω, XL = 10 Ω, XC = 20 Ω in parallel on 100 V. IR = 5 A, IL = 10 A (−j), IC = 5 A (+j). I = 5 − j10 + j5 = 5 − j5 = 7.07∠−45° A; Z = 100/7.07 = 14.14 Ω; pf = 0.707 lagging.
Memory trick Series: the bigger X wins. Parallel: the smaller X wins (it draws the bigger current). Same XL < XC → capacitive in series, inductive in parallel.

9Resonance, Q-factor & Bandwidth

high-yield
Resonance The frequency at which XL = XC, so the circuit is purely resistive (pf = 1, V and I in phase): ω₀ = 1/√(LC), f₀ = 1/(2π√(LC)). f₀ does not depend on R.

Quality factor (series): Q = ω₀L/R = 1/(ω₀CR) = (1/R)√(L/C)

Bandwidth: BW = f₀/Q = R/(2πL) Hz

Half-power (−3 dB) points f₁, f₂: I = Imax/√2, P = Pmax/2

f₀ = √(f₁f₂); for high Q, f₁,₂ ≈ f₀ ∓ BW/2

High Q → sharp peak, better selectivity

At resonanceSeries RLCParallel (tank)
Impedanceminimum, Z = Rmaximum (ideal: R; coil r: L/Cr)
Line currentmaximum, V/Rminimum
Magnificationvoltage: VL = VC = QVcurrent: IL = IC = QI
Qω₀L/RR/(ω₀L) = ω₀CR
Nameacceptor circuitrejector circuit
Below / above f₀capacitive / inductiveinductive / capacitive

Practical tank (coil r + L, with C across): f₀ = (1/2π)√(1/(LC) − r²/L²), slightly below 1/(2π√LC); dynamic impedance ZD = L/(Cr).

Worked example ✍ Series RLC: L = 10 mH, C = 1 μF, R = 10 Ω, V = 10 V.
  1. ω₀ = 1/√(10⁻² × 10⁻⁶) = 10⁴ rad/s; f₀ = 10⁴/2π = 1.59 kHz (not 10 kHz)
  2. XL = XC = ω₀L = 100 Ω; I₀ = V/R = 1 A
  3. Q = 100/10 = 10; BW = 1591/10 = 159 Hz; VL = VC = Q·V = 100 V on a 10 V supply
Memory trick Series = Small Z, Sucks current in (acceptor). Parallel = Peak Z, Pushes current away (rejector).
Exam trap f₀ ∝ 1/√(LC): C × 4 → f₀ halves. Adding R changes Q and BW, never f₀.
IT link Radio, Wi-Fi and mobile receivers tune by resonance; band-pass filters; quartz crystals (a very high-Q resonator) set CPU and RTC clocks; RFID/NFC tags resonate at 13.56 MHz.

10AC Power & Power Factor

high-yield

With v = Vm sin ωt and i = Im sin(ωt − φ): p = vi = VI cos φ − VI cos(2ωt − φ) (rms V, I). The second term averages to zero (it pulses at twice the supply frequency), so the average power is VI cos φ.

PowerFormulaUnit
Real / active PVI cos φ = I²RW
Reactive QVI sin φ = I²XVAR
Apparent SVI = I²|Z|VA
Complex SV·I* = P + jQVA
TriangleS² = P² + Q²
Power factor pf = cos φ = P/S = R/|Z|, between 0 and 1. Lagging: inductive load (motors, fans, transformers, ballasts), I lags V. Leading: capacitive load. Pure R: pf = 1, Q = 0. Pure L or C: pf = 0, P = 0. Inductive Q is positive (absorbed), capacitive Q negative.
Why low pf is bad For the same P, I = P/(V cos φ) is larger → more I²R line loss, thicker cables, bigger transformers, worse voltage drop. Utilities penalise industrial consumers with low pf.
Memory trick P does the work, Q sloshes back and forth, S is what the wires carry. Units: Watts work, VAR varies, VA is the total.
pf correction Connect a capacitor in parallel: QC = P(tan φ₁ − tan φ₂),  C = QC/(ωV²). P is unchanged; S and line current fall.
Worked example ✍ 10 kW load, pf 0.8 lagging, 220 V, 50 Hz; raise pf to 0.95.
  1. tan φ₁ = 0.75 (Q₁ = 7.5 kVAR, S₁ = 12.5 kVA); tan φ₂ = tan(cos⁻¹ 0.95) = 0.329
  2. QC = 10 000 × (0.75 − 0.329) = 4.21 kVAR; C = 4213/(314.16 × 220²) = 277 μF
  3. Line current 10 000/(220 × 0.8) = 56.8 A → 10 000/(220 × 0.95) = 47.8 A (−16%)
Exam trap A wattmeter reads P, not VI. Generators, transformers and UPSs are rated in VA because their heating depends on current, whatever the pf.
IT link A 1000 VA UPS at pf 0.6 supplies only 600 W; server PSUs use active PFC (pf > 0.9). 1 unit = 1 kWh = 3.6 MJ.
