2Voltage (Potential Difference)

Definition vab = dw/dq: the energy needed to move a unit charge from b to a. 1 V = 1 J/C. The + sign marks the higher-potential terminal, and vab = −vba.
Memory trick Water pipe: voltage = pressure, current = flow rate, resistance = narrowness of the pipe. No pressure difference → no flow. Voltage is across, current is through.
Worked examples ✍
① 120 C in 1 min → I = 120/60 = 2 A
② 3.2 mA → 3.2×10⁻³/1.6×10⁻¹⁹ = 2 × 10¹⁶ e/s
③ q = 10(1 − e⁻⁵ᵗ) mC → i = 50e⁻⁵ᵗ mA; i(0) = 50 mA
④ 30 J moves 2 C from b to a → vab = 15 V
Exam trap The SI base unit is the ampere; the coulomb is derived (A·s). Current has a direction but is a scalar: currents meeting at a node add algebraically, not as vectors.

3Power & the Passive Sign Convention

high-yield
Definition Power is the rate of energy transfer: p = dw/dt = v · i (watt). Passive sign convention: if the current enters the + terminal, p = +vi is the power absorbed; if it enters the − terminal, use p = −vi.
vi (enters at)p absorbedMeaning
5 V2 A (+)+10 Wload
5 V2 A (−)−10 Wgives 10 W
12 V3 A (+)+36 Wcharging

Power balance: in any circuit Σ p = 0: total power supplied = total power absorbed (Tellegen's theorem).

Memory trick "In at plus = it eats" (absorbs, a load). "Out at plus = it feeds" (delivers, a source). A resistor always eats: p = i²R ≥ 0.

4Energy, kWh & Efficiency

Formulas
w = ∫ p dt; constant P → W = P · t
→ 1 kWh = 3.6 × 10⁶ J = 1 "unit"
1 Wh = 3600 J · 1 hp = 746 W
η = Pout/Pin × 100%
Worked example 1 ✍ A 1.5 kW air conditioner runs 8 h a day for 30 days.
W = 1.5 × 8 × 30 = 360 kWh = 360 units; at an assumed Tk 8/unit the bill is Tk 2880.
Worked example 2: IT link ✍ Phone battery 5000 mAh at 3.7 V → 5 Ah × 3.7 V = 18.5 Wh = 18.5 × 3600 = 66.6 kJ. A 20 W charger at 85% efficiency gives 17 W → full charge ≈ 18.5/17 ≈ 1.09 h ≈ 65 min (ideal).
Worked example 3 ✍ 12 V source, 4 Ω and 2 Ω in series: I = 12/6 = 2 A. Source gives 12 × 2 = 24 W; resistors take 16 + 8 = 24 W ✓ (power balance).
Exam trap kWh is energy, not power. Do not confuse W (watt, the unit) with W (energy, the symbol). A battery being charged absorbs power: the current enters its + terminal.
Also asked 1 electron-volt = energy an electron gains through 1 V = 1.602 × 10⁻¹⁹ J · a 60 W fan for 5 h uses 0.3 kWh · 1 J = 1 W for 1 s.

7Resistance & Resistivity

Definition Resistance is the opposition to current: R = ρL/A, where ρ = resistivity (Ω·m), L = length, A = cross-section. Conductance G = 1/R in siemens (S, older ℧ "mho"); conductivity σ = 1/ρ (S/m).
Materialρ at 20 °C (Ω·m)

Copper1.7 × 10⁻⁸
Aluminium2.8 × 10⁻⁸
Nichrome (heaters)1.1 × 10⁻⁶
Temperature
Rt = R0(1 + α Δt)
Metals: α > 0 (Cu ≈ 0.0039 /°C)
Semiconductors, carbon, insulators, electrolytes: α < 0
Manganin, constantan: α ≈ 0 → standard resistors

8Ohm's Law

high-yield
Statement At constant temperature and physical conditions, the current through a conductor is directly proportional to the voltage across it: V = I R (G. S. Ohm, 1827). So I = V/R = GV and R = V/I.

Power in a resistor (always absorbed): P = VI = I²R = V²/R

Short circuit: R = 0 → v = 0 for any i. Open circuit: R = ∞ → i = 0 for any v.

Non-ohmic: diodes, transistors, thermistors, arc lamps, electrolytes, a heating filament.

Worked examples ✍
  1. Cu, 100 m, 1 mm²: R = 1.72×10⁻⁸ × 100/10⁻⁶ = 1.72 Ω; at 70 °C: ×(1 + 0.00393 × 50) ≈ 2.06 Ω
  2. Stretched to 2L: volume fixed → A/2 → R × 2 × 2 = 4R (n times → n²R)
  3. Bulb 100 W, 220 V: R = 484 Ω; on 110 V: P = 110²/484 = 25 W (½V → ¼P)
  4. 12 V on 4 Ω → 3 A · 2 A in 10 Ω → 40 W · 1 kW, 220 V heater → 48.4 Ω
Memory trick V-I-R triangle (sheet 10): cover what you want. V = I×R, I = V/R, R = V/I.
Exam trap Ohm's law is not universal (ohmic materials, constant temperature). KCL and KVL hold for every lumped circuit.

EC · Basic LawsKCL

9Nodes, Branches, Loops

  • Branch: a single element (source or resistor) between two terminals
  • Node: a point where two or more branches meet; all wire joined without an element is one node
  • Loop: any closed path that visits no node twice
  • Mesh: a loop with no other loop inside it
  • Topology: b = l + n − 1 (l = independent loops)

10Kirchhoff's Current Law (KCL)

high-yield
Statement The algebraic sum of currents at a node is zero: Σ i = 0 (entering +, leaving −), i.e. Σ iin = Σ iout. Basis: conservation of charge (a node cannot store charge). G. Kirchhoff, 1845.
Worked example 1 ✍ In = out: 5 + 3 + 1 = 2 + I₅ → I₅ = 7 A (leaving).
Worked example 2 ✍ 4 A and 2 A enter, 7 A and ix leave: 4 + 2 = 7 + ix → ix = −1 A, i.e. 1 A actually enters through that branch.
Worked example 3: closed surface ✍ KCL holds for any closed boundary (a supernode, a whole IC): three wires into a box carry 2 A in, 5 A in, so the third must carry 7 A out, whatever is inside.
Consequences Elements in series carry the same current · current sources in series must be equal · the current into a parallel group equals the sum of the branch currents · KCL at n − 1 nodes gives n − 1 independent equations (nodal analysis, lesson 2).
Memory trick KCL = Current = Charge (three Cs). KVL = Voltage = energy (the "V" points down a hill of energy).
Exam trap A negative answer is not wrong: it means the true direction is opposite to the one you assumed. Keep the sign, never "fix" it.

11Kirchhoff's Voltage Law (KVL)

high-yield
Statement The algebraic sum of all voltages around any closed loop is zero: Σ v = 0, i.e. Σ voltage rises = Σ voltage drops. Basis: conservation of energy (a charge taken round a loop returns to the same potential).
  1. Mark polarities: current enters a resistor at its + end
  2. Pick a direction and walk round the loop
  3. Meet − first → rise (−v); meet + first → drop (+v)
  4. Set the sum to 0 and solve
Worked example 1 ✍ R = 2 + 4 + 6 = 12 Ω, I = 24/12 = 2 A.
Drops: 4 V, 8 V, 12 V. KVL: −24 + 4 + 8 + 12 = 0 ✓
P: 8 + 16 + 24 = 48 W = 24 × 2 ✓
Worked example 2: opposing sources ✍ A 12 V and a 4 V source face each other in one loop with 2 Ω + 2 Ω. Net emf = 12 − 4 = 8 V → I = 8/4 = 2 A, flowing out of the 12 V + terminal and into the 4 V + terminal. 12 V source delivers 24 W; resistors take 8 + 8 = 16 W; the 4 V source absorbs 8 W (it is being charged) ✓.
Worked example 3: voltage between two points ✍ vab = sum of the drops along any path from a to b. In example 1, the voltage across R2 + R3 = 8 + 12 = 20 V, and the other path gives 24 − 4 = 20 V ✓ (path-independent).
Consequences Elements in parallel have the same voltage · series voltage sources add algebraically (aiding +, opposing −) · ideal voltage sources in parallel must be equal · KVL around the l = b − n + 1 meshes gives the mesh equations (lesson 2).
Validity Both Kirchhoff laws hold for linear and non-linear, DC and AC (instantaneous or phasor), time-varying circuits, as long as the circuit is lumped (much smaller than the wavelength).
Exam trap KVL adds voltages with sign (algebraic sum). Mixing the sign rule half-way round the loop is the most common written-exam error.

13Voltage & Current Division

Voltage divider (series)
→ vk = v · Rk/Req
two R: v₁ = v · R₁/(R₁ + R₂)
Current divider (parallel)
→ i₁ = i · R₂/(R₁ + R₂)
general: ik = i · Gk/Geq
Worked example 1 ✍ 15 V source, 3 Ω in series with (6 Ω || 3 Ω).
  1. 6 || 3 = 18/9 = 2 Ω → Req = 3 + 2 = 5 Ω → I = 15/5 = 3 A
  2. v across the pair = 3 × 2 = 6 V (divider: 15 × 2/5 = 6 V ✓)
  3. I6Ω = 3 × 3/9 = 1 A, I3Ω = 3 × 6/9 = 2 A; power 27 + 6 + 12 = 45 W = 15 × 3 ✓
Worked example 2: bulbs ✍ 100 W and 60 W (both 220 V) in series on 220 V: R = 484 Ω and 806.7 Ω, I = 220/1290.7 = 0.170 A → P100 = 14.1 W, P60 = 23.4 W: the 60 W bulb glows brighter. In parallel each gets 220 V, so the 100 W bulb is brighter.
Memory trick "Series shares the current, splits the voltage; parallel shares the voltage, splits the current." Current divider: the other resistor goes on top.
Exam trap i₁ = i · R₁/(R₁ + R₂) is wrong: the larger resistor takes the smaller current. A divider formula is only valid when no load draws current from the midpoint.

14Source Transformation

MCQ + Written
Rule A voltage source vs in series with R is equivalent (at its terminals a–b) to a current source is = vs/R in parallel with the same R, and vice versa. The current arrow points toward the + terminal.
Worked example ✍ Two practical sources, 12 V with 4 Ω and 6 V with 2 Ω, both feed a 4 Ω load in parallel. Find IL.
  1. Transform: 12/4 = 3 A || 4 Ω and 6/2 = 3 A || 2 Ω
  2. Combine: 3 + 3 = 6 A || (4 || 2 = 4/3 Ω)
  3. Back to a voltage source: 6 × 4/3 = 8 V in series with 4/3 Ω
  4. IL = 8/(4/3 + 4) = 1.5 A, vL = 6 V (check with nodal analysis, lesson 2)
Exam trap Impossible for an ideal source (R = 0 or ∞). An R in parallel with an ideal V source (or in series with an ideal I source) changes nothing at a–b.

15Wheatstone Bridge

Balance condition: P/Q = R/S (PS = QR). Then the galvanometer reads zero and the bridge needs no current measurement, only ratios.

Example ✍ P = 10 Ω, Q = 20 Ω, R = 15 Ω → S = QR/P = 20 × 15/10 = 30 Ω.
IT link: practical resistors LED on a 5 V pin, VLED = 2 V, 15 mA: R = (5 − 2)/0.015 = 200 Ω → standard 220 Ω (I ≈ 13.6 mA); P = I²R ≈ 45 mW, so a ¼ W part is ample.
Memory trick: colour code "BB ROY of Great Britain has a Very Good Wife": Black 0, Brown 1, Red 2, Orange 3, Yellow 4, Green 5, Blue 6, Violet 7, Grey 8, White 9; gold ±5%, silver ±10%. Yellow-Violet-Red-Gold = 47 × 10² = 4.7 kΩ ±5%.
