1Counting Principles, Subsets & Pigeonhole

foundation ★
Definition Counting (combinatorics) is the study of how many ways a collection of objects can be selected, arranged or distributed. n! = n(n−1)···1 counts the ways of putting n distinct objects in a row, and 0! = 1 — the one empty arrangement.
PrincipleExact statementUse it when
Addition (Sum)a task is done in m ways or n ways, never both ⇒ m + nthe cases are mutually exclusive
Multiplication (Product)a task needs m ways and then n ways ⇒ m × nthe stages happen one after another
Bijectiontwo sets have the same size iff a one-to-one pairing existsyou can rename one set as the other
The pigeonhole principle (Dirichlet) Put n objects into k boxes. If n > k, at least one box holds two or more. A "guarantee" question therefore answers n = k + 1, and some box gets at least ⌈n/k⌉.
Worked example ✍ birthdays Months are the boxes, k = 12. With 12 students every one could be born in a different month; with 13 a repeat is forced ⇒ 13. The day version needs k = 366 and gives 367.
Memory trick "OR Adds, AND Multiplies." Read the word joining the two conditions: or means add the two counts, "must happen together" means multiply.
Exam trap (i) Addition needs disjoint cases; overlapping ones need inclusion–exclusion (sheet 4). (ii) n! counts order — 5 people in 5 chairs is 5! = 120; 720 is 6!, the trap. (iii) 0! = 1. (iv) For a pigeonhole guarantee, "12 months" is not the answer.
Worked example ✍ subsets and power sets A set with n elements has exactly 2ⁿ subsets, because every element is independently in or out. It has 2ⁿ − 1 proper subsets and 2ⁿ − 1 non-empty subsets — the two counts coincide. A 5-element set therefore has 32, 31 and 31. Splitting a set into two labelled parts is also 2ⁿ.
IT link Binary representation of a k-bit string has 2ᵏ values, and the addition principle is literally how address ranges are summed: a /24 block has 2⁸ = 256 addresses, and after the network and broadcast addresses are reserved 254 remain for hosts. Choosing a bitmask with n set bits out of 32 is 32Cn, which is why subnet masks are counted that way. Pigeonhole reappears in hashing: n keys in m buckets forces a collision as soon as n > m, which is the entire reason hash tables have a load factor.

2Permutations & Combinations

most tested ★
Definition An arrangement of r objects chosen from n distinct objects, in which order matters, is a permutation: nPr = n! / (n − r)!. When order does not matter it is a combination: nCr = n! / [r! (n − r)!]. Each combination generates exactly r! orderings of its members, so nCr = nPr / r!, and nCr = nCn−r.
Question typeToolWatch out
seating, ranking, lining up, codes without repetitionnProrder matters
committee, team, hand of cards, choose r datesnCrorder irrelevant
select all r, then arrange themnCr · r!gives back nPr
objects repeated p, q, … timesn! / (p! q! …)divide by every repeat
circular seating of n people(n − 1)!rotations are identical
Memory trick "Permutation Positions — order is a position, so it counts; Combination is a Community — no chair, no order." Divide the permutation answer by r! to strip the order off: that is the size of the error.
Worked examples ✍ (a) Committee of 2 from 6: order irrelevant ⇒ 6C2 = 15. 30 = 6P2 counts ordered pairs, the standard distractor. (b) 5 books, 2 chosen and placed: 5P2 = 20; "which two" alone is 5C2 = 10. (c) 5 people in 5 chairs = 5! = 120; round a table (5−1)! = 24.
Worked example ✍ letters with repeats LEVEL: 5 letters, L twice and E twice, so distinct arrangements = 5!/(2!·2!) = 120/4 = 30. Divide once per repeated letter in BOOKKEEPER, and check the quotient is a whole number.
Exam trap (i) nCr = nPr/r!, not /n!. (ii) At r = n the combination is 1 and the permutation is n!. (iii) "Round a table" removes rotations, so divide by n. (iv) Two identical coins are indistinguishable; two people who merely look alike are not. (v) A password is a permutation even when digits repeat.

3Principle of Inclusion–Exclusion (PIE)

every MCQ uses it ★
Definition The principle of inclusion–exclusion counts the elements of a union of overlapping sets. For two sets |A ∪ B| = |A| + |B| − |A ∩ B|: adding the two sizes counts the overlap twice, so subtract it once. For three sets |A ∪ B ∪ C| = |A|+|B|+|C| − |A∩B| − |A∩C| − |B∩C| + |A∩B∩C| — the triple was added once and subtracted three times, so it must be added back once.
Quantity you wantFormula
none of the sets|U| − |A ∪ B ∪ C|
exactly one setΣ|single| − 2 Σ|pair| + 3 |triple|
at least one|U| − |A′ ∩ B′ ∩ C′|
at least twoΣ|pair| − 2 |triple|
General alternating form |A₁ ∪ … ∪ Aₙ| = Σ|Aᵢ| − Σ|Aᵢ∩Aⱼ| + Σ|Aᵢ∩Aⱼ∩Aₖ| − ⋯ + (−1)n−1Σall Aᵢ. The signs strictly alternate +, −, +, −; the k-th term combines k sets at a time.
Worked example ✍ integers 1 to 100 divisible by 3 or by 5 Let A = multiples of 3, B = multiples of 5. |A| = ⌊100/3⌋ = 33, |B| = ⌊100/5⌋ = 20, A ∩ B = multiples of 15 so |A ∩ B| = ⌊100/15⌋ = 6. Hence |A ∪ B| = 33 + 20 − 6 = 47. Trap options: 33 alone, 53 = 33 + 20 with no subtraction, 41 = 33 + 20 − 12.
Worked example ✍ none of three subjects 100 students; 45 read M, 40 read P, 35 read C; 15 read M and P, 10 read P and C, 12 read M and C; 5 read all three. |M ∪ P ∪ C| = 45 + 40 + 35 − 15 − 10 − 12 + 5 = 120 − 37 + 5 = 88, so those reading none = 100 − 88 = 12. Forgetting to add the 5 back gives 17, the standard wrong option.
Memory trick "Plus the singles, minus the pairs, plus the triples", then keep flipping the sign. An element in exactly k sets must finish counted once, and the alternating sum 1 − (k−1) + (k−2 choose 2) − ⋯ = (1 − 1)k = 0 guarantees it.
Exam trap (i) "At least one" is a union; "none" is its complement. (ii) The triple intersection is added once — dropping it is the commonest error. (iii) Write the six pairwise terms out before substituting. (iv) A universal set of size n is assumed unless stated.
Worked example ✍ exactly one subject In the class above, exactly one subject = 45 + 40 + 35 − 2(15 + 10 + 12) + 3(5) = 120 − 74 + 15 = 61. Exactly two = 15 + 10 + 12 − 3(5) = 22, and 61 + 22 + 5 = 88 with the 12 outside making 100 — a useful internal check.
Counting by complements — the habit that saves time When a question says at least one, compute none and subtract, because the "none" count is always the easier one. "Not both" = total − both; "at least one of each kind" = total − (no A) − (no B) + (no A and no B).

4PIE in Depth & Derangements

difficulty 3 favourite ★
Definition A derangement of n objects is a permutation in which no object is in its own place. Let Dₙ count them. Inclusion–exclusion applied to the n events "object i is in its place" gives Dₙ = n! (1 − 1/1! + 1/2! − 1/3! + ⋯ + (−1)ⁿ/n!), and an equivalent two-step recurrence is Dₙ = (n − 1)(Dₙ₋₁ + Dₙ₋₂).
nn!Dₙ by the formulaDₙ by (n−1)(Dₙ₋₁+Dₙ₋₂)
110—
2211 × (1 + 0) = 1
3622 × (1 + 1) = 2
42493 × (2 + 1) = 9
5120444 × (9 + 2) = 44
67202655 × (44 + 9) = 265
Worked example ✍ four letters into four addressed envelopes D₄ = 4! (1 − 1 + 1/2 − 1/6 + 1/24) = 0 + 24/2 − 24/6 + 24/24 = 0 + 12 − 4 + 1 = 9. Quick route: D₄ = 3(D₃ + D₂) = 3 × (2 + 1) = 9. Near misses: 6, 11, 23.
How to read the derangement formula Start from all n! arrangements and apply PIE to the "in its place" events: subtract the arrangements with a specified letter home, add back the pairs that are both home, subtract the triples, and so on to the n-th sign. The events are symmetric, so each k-fold intersection contains (n−k)! arrangements — that is exactly where the 1/k! terms come from.
Exam trap (i) The first two terms cancel: n! − n!/1! = 0. (ii) D₁ = 0, which is why the recurrence needs two seeds. (iii) For n ≥ 2, Dₙ is never more than n!/2. (iv) "No two letters adjacent" is a different PIE problem with blocks, and the plain formula will not give it.
IT link A spell checker or a firewall rule base avoids double counting: a packet rejected by several rules must be subtracted back into the "rejected at least once" count. The same arithmetic decides how many inputs a k-of-n threshold circuit accepts.
Worked example ✍ a derangement variant "Nobody in place, and A must not be first." Split on where A goes: it can go to position 2 or 3, and each branch contributes D₃ + D₂ = 3, so the answer is 2 × 3 = 6. This case split on one object is the standard attack on any restricted-arrangement question. Stirling numbers S(n, k) — partitions of n objects into k non-empty unordered groups — refine this further: S(n, 2) = 2ⁿ⁻¹ − 1.

5Recurrence Relations: Definition & Types

written favourite ★
Definition A recurrence relation is an equation expressing aₙ in terms of a fixed number of its preceding terms. The number of preceding terms used is the order: aₙ = aₙ₋₁ + 6aₙ₋₂ is of order 2. The independent values are the initial conditions, and a recurrence of order k needs exactly k initial values to fix the whole sequence.
ClassificationTestExample
Linearaₙ appears to the first power onlyaₙ = 2aₙ₋₁ + 3
Non-linearaₙ multiplied by itself or to a variable poweraₙ = aₙ₋₁²,   aₙ = n·aₙ₋₁
Homogeneousno term depending on n aloneaₙ = aₙ₋₁ + aₙ₋₂
Non-homogeneousa term such as 3, n or 2ⁿ appearsaₙ = 2aₙ₋₁ + n
Order 1 / order 2one / two preceding termsaₙ = 2aₙ₋₁ + 1 / Fibonacci
Constant coefficientsthe multipliers of aₙ₋₁, aₙ₋₂ … do not vary with naₙ = 3aₙ₋₁ − 2aₙ₋₂
The three solving methods 1. Iteration — write a₁, a₂, a₃ … and read the pattern. 2. Characteristic equation — for a linear homogeneous relation with constant coefficients; the standard method and the one that carries marks. 3. Generating function — replace aₙ by A(x) = Σ aₙxⁿ, multiply by xⁿ and sum, then read off a rational function whose coefficient expansion is the sequence.
Worked example ✍ iteration aₙ = aₙ₋₁ + 3 with a₀ = 2 gives 5, 8, 11 — each step adds the same 3, so aₙ = 2 + 3n. For aₙ = 2aₙ₋₁ + 3 with a₀ = 1 the values 5, 13, 29 double then add 3, which is not geometric, so shift: set bₙ = aₙ + 3, then bₙ = 2bₙ₋₁ and aₙ = 4·2ⁿ − 3.
Exam trap (i) "Homogeneous" is about the n-dependent term — aₙ = n·aₙ₋₁ is non-homogeneous. (ii) Order counts the highest lag, so aₙ = aₙ₋₂ is still order 2. (iii) Iteration is only a legal written method if the closed form is then proved by induction. (iv) A repeated root needs the extra n factor (see sheet 7).

6Characteristic-Equation Method

the standard method ★
Method For aₙ = c₁aₙ₋₁ + c₂aₙ₋₂ + ⋯ + ckaₙ₋ₖ substitute the trial solution aₙ = rⁿ. Dividing by rn−k gives the characteristic equation rk − c₁rk−1 − c₂rk−2 − ⋯ − ck = 0. Each distinct root rᵢ contributes Aᵢrᵢⁿ; a root repeated m times contributes rⁿ(A + Bn + ⋯). The constants come from the initial conditions.
Worked example ✍ a(n) = a(n−1) + 6a(n−2), a₀ = 2, a₁ = 1 Step 1 — type. Order 2, linear, homogeneous, constant coefficients ⇒ the characteristic method applies. Step 2 — characteristic equation. Put aₙ = rⁿ: rⁿ = rⁿ⁻¹ + 6rⁿ⁻² ⇒ r² = r + 6 ⇒ r² − r − 6 = 0 ⇒ (r − 3)(r + 2) = 0, so r₁ = 3, r₂ = −2, distinct. Step 3 — general solution. aₙ = A·3ⁿ + B·(−2)ⁿ. Step 4 — initial conditions. n = 0: A + B = 2. n = 1: 3A − 2B = 1. Twice the first plus the second gives 5A = 5 ⇒ A = 1, and B = 1. Step 5 — check. aₙ = 3ⁿ + (−2)ⁿ. From the recurrence a₂ = 1 + 6·2 = 13, and the formula gives 9 + 4 = 13 ✓. a₃ = 13 + 6·1 = 19, and the formula gives 27 − 8 = 19 ✓.
RecurrenceCharacteristic equationRoots

Two sequences worth memorising Arithmetic — aₙ = aₙ₋₁ + d, giving aₙ = a + nd, with Σk=1ⁿaₖ = n/2·(2a + (n−1)d). Geometric — aₙ = r·aₙ₋₁, giving aₙ = a·rⁿ, with Σk=1ⁿaₖ = a(rⁿ − 1)/(r − 1) for r ≠ 1.
Exam trap (i) Fibonacci's characteristic equation is r² − r − 1 = 0; r² − r + 1 = 0 is the classic slip. (ii) Move everything to one side first: aₙ = aₙ₋₁ + 6aₙ₋₂ gives r² − r − 6, not r² − r + 6. (iii) A repeated root needs (A + Bn)rⁿ, never Arⁿ. (iv) Order 2 means two seeds.
Where recurrences earn their keep Master-s Theorem analyses divide-and-conquer algorithms. For T(n) = 2T(n/2) + n: a = 2, b = 2, so nlog_b a = n, and f(n) = n is Θ(n) — case 2, giving T(n) = Θ(n log n). Case 1 is when f(n) dominates (merge sort), case 3 when it is polynomially smaller (binary search). Fibonacci is the cost of naive recursion, so an app reporting "how many calls" is solving a recurrence. Dynamic-programming tables (edit distance, coin change) are the same idea written bottom-up.

7Semigroups, Groups & Lagrange's Theorem

axioms asked directly ★
Definition A binary operation * on a set G assigns to every ordered pair (a, b) exactly one element of G. The four group axioms are closure (a*b ∈ G), associativity ((a*b)*c = a*(b*c)), identity (∃e with a*e = e*a = a) and inverse (∀a, ∃a⁻¹ with a*a⁻¹ = e). Add a*b = b*a and the group is Abelian. Associativity and commutativity are different properties.
StructureWhat it adds
Semigroupclosure + associativity only
Monoida semigroup with an identity element
Groupa monoid in which every element has an inverse
Abelian groupa group that is also commutative
Cyclic groupgenerated by one element g: {g⁰, g¹, …}
Subgroup H ≤ Ga subset that is itself a group under the same operation
Lagrange's theorem Let H be a subgroup of a finite group G. Then |H| divides |G|, and |G| = |H| × (number of distinct cosets). In words: the order of a subgroup must divide the order of the group, and the cosets cut G into equal pieces. Corollary: a group of prime order p has no non-trivial proper subgroup.
Worked example ✍ (ℤ, +) is an Abelian group: closure, associativity, identity 0, inverse −a, commutative. (ℤ₆, +) is a cyclic Abelian group of order 6 generated by 1; by Lagrange it has subgroups of order 1, 2 and 3 only. (ℚ*, ×) is an Abelian group with identity 1, but (ℤ*, ×) is not, since 2 has no inverse in ℤ.
Exam trap (i) Lagrange is necessity, not sufficiency: 4 divides 12, but a group of order 12 need not contain a subgroup of order 4 (A₄ does not). (ii) In (ℤ₆, +) the element 2 has order 3 and 3 has order 2. (iii) A semigroup has no identity. (iv) Closure is not automatic: on ℤ the operation − is not closed, since 1 − 2 = −1 ∉ ℤ.
Order of an element, two groups to recognise, and the crypto link The order of g is the least positive k with gk = e; in ℤₙ it is n/gcd(n, g), so ord(2) in ℤ₆ is 3 and ord(3) is 2. The symmetric group Sₙ of all permutations of n objects is a group of order n! — not Abelian for n ≥ 3, because composition reverses order. The alternating group Aₙ is the subgroup of even permutations and has order n!/2, exactly as Lagrange demands. Every public-key cipher is a group in disguise: encryption is an element of a finite group, decryption is its inverse, and security means nobody can compute that inverse without the private key. RSA works in ℤₙ*, of order φ(n).

8Rings, Integral Domains & Fields

classification table ★
Definition A ring (R, +, ×) has two binary operations: (R, +) is an Abelian group, (R, ×) is a semigroup (closed and associative, identity not required), and multiplication distributes over addition both ways. Add a multiplicative identity 1 and it is a ring with unity; add commutativity of × and it is commutative; add "no zero divisors" and it is an integral domain; add "every non-zero element is invertible" and it is a field.

Why each row reads that way ℤ fails only the field test: 2 has no inverse in ℤ. ℚ, ℝ and ℂ are fields — 1/z is the conjugate of z scaled by |z|². ℤ₆ has zero divisors (2·3 = 0) and M₂(ℝ) contains a zero matrix, so neither is a domain; GL(n) is settled by the determinant.
Memory trick "Group = one operation, and it must have an Inverse; Ring = two operations, and multiplication need not have one." Read the hierarchy downward as Field → Integral Domain → Commutative ring → ring → semigroup: the extra letters are exactly the extra conditions.
Exam trap (i) A ring need not have an identity — that is the whole difference between "ring" and "ring with unity". (ii) ℤ is an integral domain but not a field: only ±1 are units. (iii) 6 being composite is not why ℤ₆ fails; the zero divisors are. (iv) Every ring is an Abelian group under +.
Applications Cryptography. RSA works in ℤₙ under multiplication: the public key uses e with gcd(e, φ(n)) = 1, the private key d with ed ≡ 1 mod φ(n). Elliptic-curve cryptography swaps that multiplicative group for the points on a curve — still a group, with far smaller keys. Coding theory builds linear codes as subgroups of ℤ₂ⁿ, measured by minimum Hamming distance: correcting t errors needs d ≥ 2t + 1. Computer algebra works over fields so a non-zero polynomial has at most its degree many roots, which is what makes gcd and integration terminate.
